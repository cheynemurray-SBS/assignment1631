Choose one issue

Pick the one you care about most. You’ll write better when the question matters to you.

Fuel efficiency standards and safety

One recent proposal would roll back fuel efficiency standards for newer automobiles. The main argument for the move centers on “safety and saving lives.” Three lines of argument support it:

Argument A. As cars and trucks become more fuel-efficient, the cost of driving goes down, people drive more, and road fatalities increase.
Argument B. More fuel-efficient cars cost more, so people keep their cars longer and miss out on the safety features of newer cars, which increases fatalities.
Argument C. Fuel-efficient vehicles are lighter and take more damage in accidents, but they also do less damage to other vehicles. Depending on which effect is larger, fatalities could rise.

Your task: pick only one line of argument (A, B, or C), define the problem clearly, and present a data-driven argument that either defends or refutes it.

Value of an MBA degree

You’re convinced that an MBA makes monetary sense in the long run for people like you. Many of your friends and colleagues aren’t convinced.

Your task: for the benefit of your friends and colleagues, define the problem clearly and present a data-driven defense of your position.

Deceptive advertising claims

A staff attorney at the Federal Trade Commission has been assigned a deceptive-advertising case. The firm readily admits its advertising claim was false. Its defense: the claim was so blatantly false that no one would be fooled. It was meant to amuse, not deceive.

Your task: the attorney has asked you to help evaluate whether consumers were misled. Define the problem clearly and present a data-driven approach to evaluating the claim.

Six required components

Address all six, in order. Each one builds on the last, so get the problem definition right first. Select a segment to jump to that component.

Problem definition40 points totalAnalysis
1Problem definitionWhat exactly will the data show?8 pts
  • State precisely what you want to show with evidence from data and analysis
  • Write it as a specific question or hypothesis, in one or two sentences
  • Frame it in terms of measurable variables, and make it testable
  • Save the detail for later sections
Structure to followDoes [independent variable] affect [dependent variable] in [specific population or context]?
2Data and variablesWhat will you measure, and how?10 pts

Identify four to six variables that tie directly to your problem. For each one, give:

  • The variable name and an operational definition specific enough that someone else could collect it
  • Type: qualitative (nominal or ordinal) or quantitative (continuous or discrete)
  • Measurement scale: nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio
  • Units, such as dollars, years, miles per gallon, or yes/no
  • Why the variable is needed for your study
  • Three to five made-up example values
VariableDefinitionTypeScaleUnitsWhy neededExample values
AgeParticipant’s age in yearsQuantitative, discreteRatioYearsControls for age effects on driving behavior25, 34, 42, 55, 61
3Data collection planInstructions someone else could follow8 pts

Write a series of numbered instructions for the person who will actually collect the data. They should be able to follow them without asking you anything. Include:

  • Population of interest: who or what will be studied
  • Sampling method: simple random, stratified, cluster, or other, with a justification
  • Sample size: how many observations, and why that number
  • Method: survey, experiment, observation, or secondary data. For a survey, list the key questions; for an experiment, what’s manipulated and controlled
  • Timeframe: when and for how long data is collected
  • Step-by-step procedure
4Data quality justificationWhy the data will be trustworthy6 pts

This is about avoiding bias and error in collection, not just relevance. Address all five dimensions:

  • Accuracy: how you’ll keep the data correct and free from measurement error
  • Completeness: how you’ll minimize missing data
  • Consistency: how you’ll collect data the same way across every observation
  • Timeliness: whether the timeframe fits your question
  • Relevance: how the data directly answers your problem definition

Then discuss likely sources of bias and how you’ll reduce them, the reliability of your measurement methods, and validity: are you measuring what you intend to measure?

5Data display planA chart that answers the question4 pts

Describe at least one visualization:

  • The chart type, such as bar chart, histogram, scatter plot, or box plot
  • What goes on each axis
  • The comparison or pattern it reveals
  • Why it suits your data and question

A sketch is optional; hand-drawn is fine.

ExampleA side-by-side box plot comparing annual salaries (y-axis) between MBA holders and non-MBA holders (x-axis), showing the difference in earnings distributions between the two groups.
6Data analysis planWhat would prove the claim?4 pts

You haven’t studied specific analyses yet, so you don’t need to name one. Describe in plain language how the data would answer your problem:

  • What comparison you’re making: two groups, a relationship, a trend
  • What result would count as evidence for your position
  • What pattern would support your hypothesis, and what you’d need to prove or disprove
ExampleShow that the average salary of MBA holders is substantially higher than that of non-MBA holders, even after accounting for years of experience and industry, and that the difference is large enough to offset the cost and time of the program.

Common mistakes

These cost the most points on past submissions.

Unclear problem statementState it as a question in terms of specific variables, in one or two sentences.
Problem statement too longIf it runs past two sentences, the problem isn’t thought through yet. Be concise and precise.
Missing one of the six componentsUse the checklist below before you submit.
Written like a research paperUse bullets and tables where they fit. Stay concise and organized.
Variables don’t match the problemEvery variable in component 2 should clearly serve the question in component 1.
Confusing relevance with qualityComponent 4 is about avoiding bias and error in collection, not only whether the data is relevant.
Vague analysis planSay what you’d compare, what counts as evidence, and what pattern would support your hypothesis.
Missing classificationsClassify every variable as qualitative or quantitative and give its measurement scale.
No example valuesInclude three to five made-up values per variable.
Collection plan too generalWrite instructions detailed enough that someone could follow them without asking questions.

Tips and resources

Think like a data scientist

  • What would you need to measure, and how would you measure it?
  • What result would prove your point?
  • What objections or biases could someone raise, and how will you answer them?
  • Connect every variable, collection step, and analysis back to your problem.
“Measure success” becomes “measure average salary in dollars.”
“Survey people” becomes “randomly select 500 MBA graduates from the classes of 2015–2020.”
